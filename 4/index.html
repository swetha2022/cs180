Introduction

In this project, I explored the use of projective transformations and image warping to create image mosaics. The process involved capturing photographs with projective relationships between them, computing homographies from point correspondences across each pair of images, performing inverse warping using nearest neighbor and bilinear interpolation, and blending the warped images into mosaics.

Part 0: Calibrating Your Camera and Capturing a 3D Scan

Part 0.1: Calibrating Your Camera

To calibrate the camera, I first printed the ArUco calibration tags and captured 30-50 images using a phone camera, keeping the zoom consistent. While taking the pictures, I varied the angle and distance of the camera to improve calibration accuracy. Next, I wrote a script to detect the ArUco tags in each image. For each detected tag, I collected its corner coordinates and corresponding 3D world points, then used OpenCV's cv2.calibrateCamera() to compute the camera intrinsics and distortion coefficients.

Part 0.2: Capturing a 3D Object Scan

I placed my favorite object, a yellow truck, next to a printed ArUco tag and captured 30-50 images from various angles. I maintained a consistent distance so the object filled roughly half the frame and used the same camera and zoom settings as in the calibration step.

Part 0.3: Estimating Camera Pose

Using the calibrated camera parameters, I estimated the camera pose for each image. I detected the ArUco tags in each image and used OpenCV's cv2.solvePnP() function to compute the rotation and translation vectors that describe the camera's position and orientation relative to the world coordinate system defined by the tags. Part 0.4: Undistorting images and creating a dataset

I undistorted the images from Part 0.3 using the camera intrinsics and distortion coefficients computed in Part 0.1. Each image was cropped to include only valid pixels after undistortion, and the camera intrinsic matrix was updated accordingly. I then split the dataset—including images and c2w matrices—into training and validation sets (as I generated new camera poses using a single training example for testing in Part 2.6). I saved the images, c2w matrices, and the updated camera intrinsic matrix into a single .npz file for NeRF training.

Part 1: 2D Neural Field

In this section, I explored the concept of a Neural Radiance Field (NeRF) in 2D by creating a Neural Field to represent a 2D image. Since there is no radiance in 2D, the network directly maps pixel coordinates to RGB color values. I implemented a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with 4 fully connected layers (3 hidden layers with ReLU activations and 1 output layer with Sigmoid) to map 2D pixel coordinates to RGB colors. To better capture high-frequency details in the image, I applied Sinusoidal Positional Encoding (PE) to expand the input coordinates into a higher-dimensional space. To efficiently train the network, I implemented a dataloader that randomly samples a batch of pixel coordinates and their corresponding colors at each iteration. Inputs and outputs were normalized to the range [0, 1] to ensure stable training. The network was trained using mean squared error (MSE) loss and optimized with the Adam optimizer at a learning rate of 0.01. Performance was monitored using PSNR to evaluate the reconstruction quality. I experimented with different network widths and maximum frequencies for positional encoding to study their effect on reconstruction quality, specifically testing widths of 64 and 256 and maximum frequencies of 2 and 10. During training, I visualized intermediate outputs to monitor the network's progression and compared final results across these hyperparameter choices. I found that a width of 256 and a maximum frequency of 10 produced the best reconstructions.

Introduction

In this project, I explored the fascinating world of diffusion models, which have revolutionized image generation in recent years. The project was divided into two main parts: first working with pre-trained diffusion models to understand the denoising process, and then implementing my own diffusion model from scratch. I began by experimenting with DeepFloyd IF, a state-of-the-art text-to-image model, to generate images from text prompts and perform various manipulations. I implemented forward and reverse diffusion processes, learning how noise is progressively added and removed. I then explored techniques like Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG) to improve image quality and image-to-image translation to transform existing images. Finally, I built and trained my own diffusion model on the MNIST dataset, implementing both unconditional and conditional generation, and even created a class-conditional diffusion model capable of generating specific digits on demand. Through this project, I gained deep insights into how modern generative AI systems work and the mathematics underlying diffusion processes.

Part 1.1: Implementing the Forward Process

The forward diffusion process gradually adds Gaussian noise to an image over a series of timesteps. I implemented this by using the closed-form solution that allows us to directly compute a noisy image at any timestep t without iterating through all previous steps. The formula uses pre-computed noise schedule coefficients to blend the original image with random noise. I tested this on the "Campanile" test image provided, creating noisy versions at timesteps t = [250, 500, 750]. As expected, the image becomes progressively noisier at higher timesteps, eventually becoming almost pure noise.

Part 1.2: Classical Denoising

To understand the challenge of denoising, I applied Gaussian blur filtering to the noisy images from Part 1.1. While Gaussian blur can remove some noise, it also significantly reduces image sharpness and detail. The results show that classical denoising methods are insufficient for heavily noised images, motivating the need for learned diffusion models that can intelligently reconstruct image structure.

Part 1.3: One-Step Denoising

Next, I used the pretrained diffusion model's UNet to estimate the noise in the noisy images and attempted single-step denoising. The UNet predicts the noise component at each timestep, which can then be removed to recover a cleaner image. However, one-step denoising from heavily corrupted images (high timesteps) produces blurry, low-quality results because the model needs multiple refinement steps to progressively recover fine details. The results are better than Gaussian blur but still far from the original image quality.

Part 1.4: Iterative Denoising

To achieve high-quality reconstruction, I implemented the iterative denoising procedure. Starting from a noisy image, the algorithm repeatedly estimates and removes noise, gradually refining the image over multiple timesteps. I used a strided timestep sequence (every 30 steps from 990 down to 0) to efficiently denoise while maintaining quality. The results show dramatic improvement compared to one-step denoising, successfully recovering the original Campanile image from pure noise. I also visualized intermediate denoising steps at timesteps [690, 540, 390, 240, 90] to show the progressive refinement.

Part 1.5: Diffusion Model Sampling

With the iterative denoising procedure working, I could now generate completely new images from pure random noise. Starting from Gaussian noise, the model progressively denoises to create images matching the conditioning prompt. I generated five images using the prompt "a high quality photo" and visualized the denoising trajectory for one of them. Part 1.6: Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)

To improve image quality and adherence to text prompts, I implemented Classifier-Free Guidance. CFG works by combining conditional and unconditional noise predictions, extrapolating in the direction of the conditional prediction. The guidance scale parameter controls this effect: higher values produce images that match the prompt more closely but may sacrifice diversity. I experimented with guidance scales of [1, 5, 7, 10] for the prompt "a high quality photo". Results show that moderate guidance (around 7) provides the best balance of quality and coherence.

Part 1.7: Image-to-Image Translation

I implemented image-to-image translation by starting the denoising process from a noisy version of an existing image rather than pure noise. The i_start parameter controls how much noise is added initially—smaller values preserve more of the original structure, while larger values allow more creative freedom. I tested this with the Campanile image and various prompts, using different i_start values of [1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 20]. The results show how the balance between faithfulness to the original and adherence to the new prompt changes with noise level.

Part 1.7.1: Editing Hand-Drawn and Web Images

I tested image-to-image translation on hand-drawn sketches and web images. This demonstrates the model's ability to transform simple line drawings into photorealistic images and to re-imagine existing photographs in new styles or contexts.

Part 1.7.2: Inpainting

I implemented inpainting by creating a binary mask indicating which regions to regenerate. During the denoising loop, I replace the masked region with the model's prediction while keeping the unmasked region from the original (noisy) image. This allows selective editing of images while preserving the rest of the content.

Part 1.7.3: Text-Conditional Image-to-Image Translation

I experimented with creative text prompts to transform images in interesting ways. By carefully crafting prompts and tuning the noise level, I could achieve various artistic effects while maintaining recognizable elements from the source image.

Part 1.8: Visual Anagrams

Visual anagrams are images that look like one thing when viewed normally but reveal a different image when flipped upside down. I implemented this by denoising with two different text prompts simultaneously: one for the normal orientation and one for the flipped orientation. At each denoising step, I estimate the noise for both prompts, flip the noise estimate for the second prompt, and average them together. This forces the model to find an image that satisfies both prompts at once. I experimented with several prompt pairs including "an oil painting of an old man" / "an oil painting of people around a campfire", "an oil painting of a snowy mountain village" / "a rocket ship", and my own creative prompt pair.

Part 1.9: Hybrid Images

Hybrid images are optical illusions that appear as one image when viewed up close but reveal a different image when viewed from far away. This works by combining the high-frequency components of one image with the low-frequency components of another. I implemented this by denoising with two different prompts simultaneously, applying a low-pass filter to the noise estimate from the first prompt and a high-pass filter to the noise estimate from the second prompt, then combining them. The result is an image that looks like the high-frequency prompt when viewed closely and transitions to the low-frequency prompt when viewed from a distance. I tested this with prompt pairs including "a skull" / "a man wearing a hat", "a lithograph of waterfalls" / "a lithograph of a skull", and my own creative combination.

Part 2: Training a Diffusion Model from Scratch

Part 2.1: Training a Single-Step Denoising UNet

I began building my own diffusion model by implementing and training a UNet to denoise MNIST digits in a single step. The UNet uses a series of downsampling and upsampling blocks with skip connections. I trained it using L2 loss between the predicted and true noise, optimizing with the Adam optimizer. After training for 5 epochs with a batch size of 128 and learning rate of 1e-4, the model learned to denoise images reasonably well, though results were somewhat blurry due to the single-step limitation.

Training hyperparameters:

  • Epochs: 5
  • Batch size: 128
  • Learning rate: 1e-4
  • Optimizer: Adam
  • Hidden dimension: 128

Denoising Results After Training

Part 2.2: Training a Diffusion Model

I extended the single-step model to a full diffusion model capable of iterative denoising. The key modification was conditioning the UNet on the timestep t by adding learned time embeddings to the hidden representations. I trained this time-conditioned UNet for 20 epochs, sampling noise levels uniformly during training. The model learned to denoise at various noise levels, enabling iterative refinement.

Training hyperparameters:

  • Epochs: 20
  • Batch size: 128
  • Learning rate: 1e-4
  • Optimizer: Adam
  • Hidden dimension: 128

Part 2.3: Adding Class-Conditioning

To enable controlled generation of specific digits, I added class conditioning to the UNet. I created one-hot encoded class vectors and concatenated them with the time embeddings before injecting into the network. During training, I randomly dropped the class conditioning 10% of the time to enable classifier-free guidance at sampling time. This allows the model to generate specific digits on demand while maintaining generation quality.

Training hyperparameters:

  • Epochs: 20
  • Batch size: 128
  • Learning rate: 1e-4
  • Optimizer: Adam
  • Hidden dimension: 128
  • Unconditional probability: 0.1
